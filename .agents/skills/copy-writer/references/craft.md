# Craft Principles and Anti-Patterns

The working knowledge of a senior web copywriter. Apply these when drafting and when
pressure-testing your own work.

## The three pillars

Every piece of interface copy should do three things. These sit underneath everything
below.

### Empathy, meet the user's emotional state
Recognize how the user feels at this moment, anxious, frustrated, rushed, skeptical, 
and respond with clear, human language. Don't blame, don't lecture, don't perform.

**Tone caveat.** Empathy is a *stance*, not a *volume*. Adopt the principle, meet the
user where they are, but express it in the brand's own voice. A calm brand shows empathy
with a steady, useful line, not cheerleading. See `app-context.md` for how this brand
sounds.

### Anticipation, answer the question before it's asked
Place just-in-time information in front of people so they never feel stranded. Three
moves:

- **Progress in advance**, "Step 1 of 3," "about 2 minutes," "no credit card
  required." Predictability reduces friction.
- **Preemptive reassurance**, dissolve the unspoken worry: "You can change this later."
- **Answer the unasked**, "We'll only use this to contact you. No list, no sharing."

See `ux-patterns.md` for the patterns.

### Transparency, say what happens, when, and what it costs
Be upfront about cost, timing, and consequences *before* the user commits. Hidden fees,
surprise steps, and vague outcomes breed suspicion; spelled-out ones build trust. "Your
free trial ends June 15, cancel anytime before then, no charges." Never let the user
discover a cost or a catch at the last step.

## Principles

### Lead with the user's world, not the product's
Open with the reader's situation, question, or desire, not with the feature or the
system. A question ("Who's in your house?") pulls people in; an instruction ("Build your
house") pushes them.

### Give a reason, not a disclaimer
Never tell users something is "optional," "you can skip this," or "no pressure" unless
the genuine goal is to lower anxiety. Disclaimers signal *this doesn't matter* and
suppress completion. Replace them with a benefit that motivates the action: "The more you
share, the more your family gets out of it." Keep the reassurance that reduces fear
("you can change it later") without granting blanket permission to skip.

### Say the true, specific thing
Concrete nouns and real verbs beat abstractions. "Pets underfoot," "anyone who lives
elsewhere" land; "the people, the pets, the place" is a rhythm, not a meaning. Specificity
is what makes copy feel written by a human who understands the product.

### Respect the reader's time and intelligence
Short, direct, no padding. Don't over-explain, don't talk down, don't state the obvious.
Every word should earn its place. If a sentence survives deletion without loss, delete it.
Prefer active voice and short sentences; cut adverbs and qualifiers. Read it as a tired
person would, at a glance.

### Sound like a person
Contractions, plain words, natural rhythm, varied sentence length. Read it aloud. If it
sounds assembled from a template, rewrite it.

### One idea per element
A headline headlines. A subhead supports or adds a distinct angle. A CTA commands. Don't
make one line do three jobs, and don't let two elements say the same thing.

### Match the moment's emotional register
The same brand speaks differently at sign-up, at an error, and at a milestone. Match the
register to the user's state: reassure the anxious, energize the excited, respect the
rushed.

### Earn the action
A CTA should be a clear verb with a clear outcome, and the surrounding copy should have
made the action feel worth taking. Never rely on urgency or pressure to compensate for a
weak value proposition.

## Anti-patterns

The tells of AI-generated or lazy copy. Hunt them actively in your own drafts.

### Undercutting disclaimers
- "Everything here is optional."
- "You can skip this."
- "No pressure, do whatever works for you."
Use only when reducing anxiety is the actual goal, and even then prefer a benefit.

### Stacked hedges and filler adverbs
"Simply," "just," "easily," "seamlessly," "effortlessly," "quickly." They add nothing and
often imply the user is slow for finding it hard.

### Empty intensifiers
"Powerful," "robust," "delightful," "amazing," "world-class," "cutting-edge," "next-
generation." They assert quality instead of showing it.

### Restating the heading in the subhead
"Getting to know your family" → "Here you'll get to know your family." The subhead must
add information, not echo.

### Symmetry for its own sake
"the people, the pets, the place." Triads and parallel structures feel tidy but often
trade meaning for rhythm. Use them only when the parallel is real.

### Explaining the UI instead of speaking to the user
"This field is where you enter your address." Speak to the person: "Where do you live?"

### Uniform rhythm
Sentences of the same length and shape, paragraph after paragraph. Human writing varies.

### Over-hedging and corporate distance
"We strive to," "we aim to," "in order to," "please be advised." Say it directly.

### Feature-first framing
Opening with what the product does rather than what the user gets. Lead with the outcome.

## Know whether it works

Copy is a hypothesis, not a verdict. A senior writer proposes how to tell if it landed:

- **Name the signal.** What would show this copy worked, completion, click-through,
  fewer support replies, less hesitation?
- **Offer a variant.** When the choice is a genuine judgment call, give a second option
  and say what each one optimizes for, so it can be tested.
- **Watch for friction.** Long hovers, repeated reads, and abandoned steps are copy
  problems as often as design problems.
- **Iterate.** Even seasoned writers A/B a single phrase. Treat the first draft as a
  starting point, not a deliverable.

## Pressure-test checklist

Before delivering, ask:

- Is it clear, kind, and necessary right now? (the three-question test)
- Does it do the job I wrote in the brief?
- Would the target user understand it in one read, in context?
- Is every word earning its place?
- Does it sound like a person, or like a template?
- Does it avoid every anti-pattern above?
- Does it fit the layout, length, and any constraints?
- Is it consistent with the surrounding copy and the brand voice?
